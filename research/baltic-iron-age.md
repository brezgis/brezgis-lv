# Baltic Iron Age Village Reconstruction: Archaeological Research Brief

**Site context:** Eastern Vidzeme Upland, Latvia (Taurene parish, near Vecpiebalga/Dzērbene), ~25-30 km from Āraiši and Cēsis.
**Purpose:** Grounding data for an archaeologically defensible 3D reconstruction.
**Compiled:** 2026-07-04

---

## 1. CHRONOLOGY, PEOPLES, AND THE VERDICT

### Latvian Iron Age Periodization

The Latvian Iron Age runs from ~1 AD to ~1200 AD and is divided into three periods [1][2]:

- **Early Iron Age (Agrais dzelzs laikmets):** AD 1-400
- **Middle Iron Age (Vidējais dzelzs laikmets):** AD 400-800
- **Late Iron Age (Vēlais dzelzs laikmets):** AD 800-1200

Iron objects (imported) appear in Latvia by the 5th century BC, but local smelting begins only around the turn of the Common Era [1].

### Who Lived in Eastern Vidzeme in Each Period

**Early Iron Age (~AD 1-400):** The population in eastern Vidzeme during this period is poorly understood but almost certainly included a Finnic-speaking component alongside Baltic-speaking groups. The key evidence is tarand graves -- rectangular stone-chambered collective burial structures characteristic of Finnic peoples -- which extend across northern and eastern Latvia during the Roman Iron Age [3][4]. These graves feature cremation with ashes dispersed in the stone chambers. The Balts in this area practiced collective inhumation in sand barrows [3]. Population density in the Vidzeme Upland was low; settlement archaeology for this period is extremely sparse, and most evidence comes from burials rather than habitation sites [4][5]. The region would have been heavily forested with scattered small agricultural clearings. Trade connections with the Roman world are attested by imported goods (glass beads, bronze fibulae) but the upland interior was peripheral -- the main trade routes followed rivers (Daugava, Gauja) [2].

**Middle Iron Age (~AD 400-800):** This is when distinct Baltic tribal identities crystallize. The Latgalians emerge as a recognizable group in eastern Vidzeme from the 5th-6th centuries, occupying the territory west of the Aiviekste River [6]. They develop from earlier local Baltic populations, identifiable by their hillforts and increasingly rich grave goods reflecting chieftain hierarchies [6]. The Finnic presence does not vanish but becomes archaeologically less visible; a "hidden Finnic presence" is suggested by place-name evidence and is confirmed textually as late as 1179/80 when "the Chud in Ochela" (Atzele) are mentioned in eastern Latvia [4]. Lake fortresses begin to appear in this period (from the 7th century onward) [7].

**Late Iron Age (~AD 800-1200):** Eastern Vidzeme is firmly Latgalian territory. This is the period of maximum archaeological visibility: hillforts as chieftain seats, lake fortresses (10 discovered in Latvia), rich flat cemeteries and barrow burials, intensive agriculture, established trade routes with Scandinavia and Rus', and complex social stratification [6][7][8]. The Cēsis area (Riekstu Hill) shows a pre-Crusade Latgalian hillfort dominating the regional landscape [9].

### The Verdict: AD ~835-1000 (Late Iron Age, 9th-10th century)

**This is the richest, most defensible period for reconstruction, and it is not even close.** The reasons:

1. **Archaeological density.** We have Āraiši -- excavated to extraordinary detail, 25 km away, same cultural group, same period, with 3,500+ artifacts and building remains preserved to multiple log courses [8][10].
2. **Ethnographic identity.** The people are securely identified as Latgalians with a well-characterized material culture, burial practices, costume, and economy [6][11].
3. **Precision dating.** The Āraiši platform is dendro-dated to AD 835 (felling date) via the Miyake event of AD 774/5 [12]. This anchors our reconstruction to a specific generation.
4. **Completeness.** Between Āraiši finds, regional cemetery excavations (Nukši, Kristapiņi, Odukalns), and hillfort data, we can reconstruct buildings, clothing, food, tools, and ritual with reasonable confidence.

For AD ~50 (Early Iron Age): the scene is legitimate but far more speculative. You would show sparse Baltic/Finnic settlement in heavy forest, perhaps a small cluster of log structures near a field clearing, with tarand-grave cemeteries and very limited material culture evidence. It works as a "wilderness with people in it" scene but cannot be populated with the same architectural and cultural specificity.

**My recommendation:** Use AD ~850-950 as the primary reconstruction date. If you also want to show AD ~50 for contrast (e.g., the aurochs-era scene), depict it honestly as near-wilderness with minimal human presence.

## 2. ĀRAIŠI LAKE FORTRESS DEEP DIVE

### Dating

- Original excavation dating (Apals, by artifact typology): late 1st millennium AD, "9th-10th centuries" [8]
- Dendrochronology: a ~100-year floating chronology for Norway spruce (Picea abies) timbers from Phase I [12]
- Radiocarbon wiggle-match (2014): initial estimate placed construction ~AD 780 [12]
- **High-precision single-year 14C dating (2023):** A spruce log from the timber platform synchronized with the AD 774/5 Miyake event. Felling date: **AD 835**. This is 50-60 years later than the earlier wiggle-match estimate [13]
- The platform and Phase I central houses were built from timbers felled in a single year; surrounding Phase I buildings from timbers felled 1-2 years later [12]
- Five successive building phases, final destruction/abandonment ~11th century by hostile attack and burning [10][14]

### Site Layout and Dimensions

- **Platform:** approximately **40 x 40 meters** (~1,600 m2), raised ~1 meter above water level [14]
- **Foundation:** rectangular framework of pine and spruce log grates (lattice), covered with a spread of horizontal logs [10][14]
- **Island:** a low, frequently flooded moraine island connected to shore by an **80-meter-long natural bank of glacial till** [10]
- **Buildings per phase:** approximately **16 houses**, population **50-90 people** [10][14]
- **Building arrangement:** close together in rows along the perimeter of the platform, with an extra row along the middle. Streets between rows were "very narrow" [14]
- **Defensive works:** two perimeters of ramparts in the form of a **palisade** with a passageway/path between them for movement of guards, located along the platform margin [10][14]. A defensive wall fragment and gate were reconstructed in 2020 [8]
- **Access:** narrow shallow area with wooden paving connecting to the stony bank/causeway [14]

### Building Construction

- **Type:** single-room chimney-less dwelling with a porch in the end wall [10]
- **Interior area:** most residential buildings had an internal area of **up to 30 square meters** (~5x6 m or similar) [14]
- **Wall construction -- two methods documented** [14]:
  - *Log technique (primary):* horizontally stacked logs connected at corners with **tongue-and-groove joints** (not simple saddle notches). Oak pins joined stacked beams to prevent shifting. Gaps sealed with straw, tow (linen fiber), or dried moss.
  - *Yoke technique (older method):* two vertical posts with ends leading to a yoke preventing twisting, with horizontal wall beams inserted between posts. (This is sometimes called the "yoke corner joint" or pillar-and-beam construction.)
- **Preservation:** lower sections of buildings preserved to **4-5 logs high** in waterlogged conditions; some structures up to 7 logs high [8][14]
- **Reconstruction reliability:** Apals assessed **80% reliability** for the earliest building phase based on preserved structural remains [8]

### Roof

- **Form:** low **gable roofs** with ridges made of massive, long logs [14]
- **Materials:** round logs or split planks as rafters, covered with **birch or spruce bark sheets**, weighted down by timbers [10]. Some structures may have incorporated sod for additional weight [10]

### Hearth / Stove

- **A clay stove** positioned **at the center of the living room** [10]
- Sleeping areas and benches arranged along the walls around the stove [10]
- The structure was chimney-less (smoke exited through the door, roof gaps, or a smoke-hole) [10]

### Finds and Daily Life

- **Total finds:** ~3,500-3,700 artifacts; ~100,000 pottery fragments; remains of ~150 wooden buildings across all phases [8][10]
- **Organic preservation:** wood, bone, and other organic materials preserved exceptionally in waterlogged anaerobic conditions -- including objects that normally decompose (wooden tools, bone combs, textile fragments) [8]
- **Specific finds mentioned:** bone and bristle combs for grooming; wooden mallets for washing clothes; crucibles and semi-finished jewelry products (indicating an on-site jeweler); clay vessels (over 100 intact or largely intact) [10][14]
- **Economy:** agriculture, cattle breeding, forest apiculture (beekeeping), craftsmanship, trade, fishing, and hunting [10][14]
- **Diet:** cereal crops (rye primarily, also wheat and barley), turnips, meat, milk, wild game, and fish [10][14]
- **Destruction:** settlement attacked, burned to the ground, and abandoned; foundation sank as water level rose [10][14]

### The Modern Reconstruction

- Reconstruction carried out 1981-1995 and continuing [8]
- 14 reconstructed buildings of Phase I displayed to visitors [8]
- Built using replicas of ancient tools and authentic building techniques [8]
- Chosen for reconstruction: the earliest (Phase I) building phase from the 9th century, as best preserved [8]
- Lower building sections recreated as copies of recovered originals; upper parts reconstructed using ethnographic parallels [8]
- Member of EXARC (European network of open-air archaeological museums) [8]
- Adjacent "Meitu Island" features reconstructions of Stone Age and Bronze Age dwellings for comparison [8]

## 3. LAND SETTLEMENTS AND FARMSTEAD LAYOUT

### The Evidence Problem

Land settlements are far less well-excavated than lake fortresses, hillforts, or cemeteries in Latgalian territory. As the Wikipedia article on Latgalians notes: "Few dwelling sites have been excavated compared to burial grounds" [6]. Our picture of land farmsteads draws on: (a) the lake fortress data as a structural parallel for log building techniques; (b) hillfort excavation data; (c) ethnographic documentation of Latvian farmsteads which shows strong continuity from at least the 10th century; and (d) comparative Lithuanian data from unenclosed settlements.

### Farmstead Layout (Late Iron Age, ~9th-11th century)

Written evidence confirms farmsteads with **several buildings used for different purposes as early as the 10th century** [15]. The layout principles documented ethnographically appear to have deep roots:

- **Dwelling house (māja/istaba):** built in a dry, elevated place, oriented lengthwise north-to-south. Log construction identical to Āraiši techniques (horizontal logs, corner joints, gable roof). Single-room or at most two-room. Clay stove inside [10][15].
- **Granary (klēts):** positioned **south of the dwelling, closer than other outbuildings**. Ethnographic evidence shows granaries raised on posts or stone footings to protect grain from moisture and rodents. Used for storing grain, valuables, and sometimes sleeping in summer. A klēts on posts is archaeologically plausible for the Late Iron Age given the technology [15].
- **Cattle byre / shed:** placed **north of the dwelling at a distance** [15].
- **Threshing barn (rija):** situated on a hillock away from the farmstead, accounting for wind patterns for winnowing [15].
- **Bathhouse (pirts):** located near water. While the archaeological evidence for the pirts in this period is thin, the practice of steam-bathing is ancient in Baltic culture and the word is Proto-Baltic [15].
- **All buildings had to be visible from the windows or doors of the main house** -- a principle of oversight and security [15].
- **Buildings arranged compactly** to minimize agricultural land use [15].

### Fences

Wattle fences and post-and-rail fences are consistent with the technology level. Split-rail and wattle construction requires only an axe and flexible withies (hazel, willow). While no excavated Late Iron Age Latvian fence posts have been specifically published to my knowledge, fencing is a logical necessity for livestock management and the materials are the same as those used in building walls.

### Relationship to Hillforts

Hillforts (pilskalns) were **part of a complex consisting of the main fortress, the settlement around it, one or more burial fields, and nearby ritual sites** [9][16]. During the Late Iron Age (10th-11th centuries), some hillforts became military fortresses with strong fortifications, serving as seats of local chiefs [16]. The typical farmer lived in an unfortified farmstead within the hillfort's territorial influence, using the hillfort as a refuge in times of attack. The Cēsis hillfort (Riekstu Hill, rising 18 m above surroundings) controlled the regional countryside near trade routes [9].

### Regional Note: Latgale vs. Vidzeme Settlement Patterns

In Latgale proper (eastern Latvia), farmsteads grouped into village clusters. In Vidzeme, the pattern tends toward more dispersed (scattered) single-family farmsteads, which became the characteristic Latvian viensēta [15]. For our reconstruction site in the eastern Vidzeme Upland, a scattered farmstead pattern is appropriate.

## 4. SUBSISTENCE AND FOOD

### Crops

From the Āraiši evidence and broader Latvian Late Iron Age data [10][14][17]:

- **Rye (Secale cereale):** the dominant crop. "Plenty of rye" at Āraiši [14]. Rye's transition from weed to primary crop in the Baltic was favored by colder Iron Age climate and permanent field culture [17].
- **Barley (Hordeum vulgare):** attested, "a little" [14]. The earliest macrobotanical evidence for crop cultivation in the southeastern Baltic is barley grains dated ~1300-1250 cal BC [17].
- **Wheat (Triticum aestivum/durum):** attested, "a little" [14].
- **Oats (Avena sativa):** likely present by the Late Iron Age (common in contemporary Scandinavian/Baltic contexts) though not specifically named in the Āraiši sources I found.
- **Flax (Linum usitatissimum):** highly probable -- linen textiles are documented from grave finds, and linen tow (fiber) was used as building caulking at Āraiši [14].
- **Peas (Pisum sativum):** attested in the broader southeastern Baltic from 1000-400 cal BC onward [17].
- **Turnips (Brassica rapa):** explicitly mentioned in the Āraiši dietary evidence [14].

### Agriculture: Slash-and-Burn vs. Permanent Fields

Both systems coexisted in Iron Age Latvia. Palynological (pollen) evidence from the landscape around Cēsis shows "little evidence for sustained agricultural activity during the late Iron Age" with substantial woodland remaining [18]. This suggests the upland areas were still largely forested, with cultivation in clearings.

Slash-and-burn (lyderudzis in Latvian) is indicated by charred particles, cereal pollen, decline of long-living shade trees, and increase of short-living pioneer species in the pollen record [17]. However, by the Late Iron Age, permanent fields near settlements were standard -- Āraiši's inhabitants "began intensive cultivation of land around Lake Āraiš" [14]. The likely pattern: permanent infields near the farmstead (rye, barley) supplemented by slash-and-burn outfields in the forest for occasional cropping.

### Livestock

**Cattle:** The primary domestic animal. Iron Age Baltic cattle were **small** -- withers height approximately 100-118 cm based on European Iron Age zooarchaeological data (compared to ~140+ cm for modern breeds) [19]. These were short-legged, multi-purpose animals providing milk, draft power, and eventually meat. Dark or mixed coloring is likely based on primitive breed parallels. **Your depiction of small cattle is correct.**

**Sheep/Goat:** Present in faunal assemblages. Iron Age sheep were small, dark, probably similar to surviving primitive northern European breeds (Soay-type, though not necessarily that specific breed). Wool was a major textile resource. **Dark primitive sheep: correct.**

**Pig (Sus domesticus):** Well-attested. Pigs increased in importance through the Iron Age. They are efficient forest-edge animals suited to the wooded landscape [19].

**Horse:** Extremely important in Baltic culture -- horse bones constitute the second-largest or even largest portion of faunal material at some western Latvian and Lithuanian sites [19]. Horses were small, **pony-sized** -- approximately 126 cm at withers for Iron Age European horses generally [20]; the native Baltic type (ancestral to the Žemaitukas) was likely 125-140 cm [21]. The Žemaitukas is documented from the 6th-7th centuries and was used as a war-horse during the Baltic Crusades [21]. Horses were buried with male warriors in graves, indicating high cultural value [11]. **Tarpan-like horses (small, sturdy, dun/dark): a reasonable depiction.** The Žemaitukas type is the closest documented native breed.

**Chicken (Gallus gallus domesticus):** **Present but recent.** In neighboring Estonia, the earliest confirmed chicken bone is from Rebala, dated **200 cal BCE - 5 cal CE** [22]. Chicken husbandry became "well established by the later part of the Viking Age" (9th-10th century) with remains comprising 2-8% of faunal assemblages [22]. In Lithuania, chicken bones appear at Vilnius Lower Castle in 5th-9th century contexts [22]. For Latvia specifically, the question "has not been addressed" archaeologically [22], but given the Estonian and Lithuanian evidence, **chickens are plausible for a 9th-10th century Latgalian farmstead, though they would be few and small** -- not the focus of the farm economy. **Your depiction of chickens is defensible for ~AD 950 but should be modest** (a handful of birds, not a large flock).

**Dog:** Universally present; working animals for herding and guarding.

### Fishing, Hunting, Beekeeping

- **Fishing:** Explicitly attested at Āraiši (lake settlement!) [14]. Nets, fish spears, and hooks known from the broader Baltic archaeological record.
- **Hunting:** Attested at Āraiši [14]. Wild game supplemented the diet. Target species: elk, wild boar, red deer, roe deer, beaver, water birds, hare.
- **Forest beekeeping (dravniecība):** Explicitly mentioned as an economic activity at Āraiši ("forest apiculture") [14]. This involved maintaining hollow trees as wild bee nests, a practice documented across the medieval Baltic and Eastern Europe. Latvia's beekeeping tradition dates to prehistory [23]. Mead and honey were significant products.

### Food Preparation

- **Hearth/stove:** clay stove in center of dwelling; chimney-less (the "black kitchen" or dūmu istaba tradition) [10]
- **Vessels:** hand-built pottery (no potter's wheel until ~11th century in Latvia) fired at 600-700 C in open bonfires [24]. Large vessels for storage; smaller for cooking.
- **Porridge/gruel (putra):** the staple food -- barley or rye grains boiled in water or milk in clay pots over the stove.
- **Bread:** likely flatbreads baked on hot stones or in ashes; oven-baked raised bread develops later [25].
- **Beer (alus) and mead (medalus/miestiņš):** both brewed before the 13th century from grains, honey, and possibly flavored with herbs [25]. Fundamental to social and ritual life.
- **Meat:** boiled in pots or roasted. Evidence of butchering at hillforts shows systematic processing [19].
- **Food storage:** the klēts granary for grain; salting and smoking for meat/fish; root cellars for turnips.

## 5. CRAFTS AND MATERIAL CULTURE

### Pottery

- Hand-built throughout the Middle and Late Iron Age (5th-12th centuries) [24]
- Organic inclusions (chaff, grass) used as temper in clay mixture for large vessels [24]
- Fired without kilns in open bonfires at 600-700 C [24]
- Surface treatment: striated/roughened exterior common in the "Baltic Ware" tradition
- **Potter's wheel introduced ~11th century** [24] -- so for a 9th-10th century scene, ALL pottery is hand-formed
- Vessel types: large storage pots, cooking pots, smaller bowls; some vessels have pot lids [24]
- At Āraiši: ~100,000 pottery fragments and over 100 intact/nearly intact vessels recovered [8]

### Iron Smelting

- Based on **bog iron ore** (limonite nodules from wetlands) [26]
- Smelted in **bloomery furnaces** at 1100-1300 C [26]
- Iron content of analyzed Latvian ores: 43-52% [26]
- The bloom (spongy iron mass) was hammered to consolidate into wrought iron [26]
- Yield: 10-20% of ore mass converted to usable iron [26]
- Products: axes, knives, spearheads, arrowheads, sickles, needles, fire-steels
- An iron industry based on bog ore existed particularly in southern and western Latvia; the Vidzeme Upland with its numerous bogs would have had local access to ore [26]

### Bronze Ornament Casting

- Crucibles and semi-finished bronze products found **at Āraiši** -- an on-site jeweler was working there [10]
- Latgalian bronze work features elaborate geometric abstraction [27]
- Techniques: lost-wax casting, sheet bronze working, wire-drawing for spirals
- Products: brooches (fibulae), arm rings, finger rings, pendants, crown headbands (vaiņags), decorative mounts

### Weaving and Textiles

- Warp-weighted loom (standard Baltic/Northern European type)
- Wool and linen textiles documented from Latgalian grave finds [11]
- Tablet weaving for decorative bands [11]
- Villaine (shoulder shawl/cloak) decorated with bronze ornaments [11][27]

### Woodwork

- **Dugout canoes (vienkoči):** essential for lake/river transport. Known from Baltic archaeological contexts throughout prehistory [28]
- Turned and carved wooden vessels, ladles, mallets [10]
- Building tools: axes (the primary tool), adzes, drawknives, chisels
- At Āraiši: wooden mallets for laundry, various wooden implements preserved in waterlogged conditions [10]

### Objects Visible in a Yard

A defensible 3D scene of a farmstead yard should show: an axe and chopping block; a quern (hand-mill) for grinding grain; clay pots in various sizes; a wooden trough; fishing nets drying; split firewood; a loom (possibly under an awning); spindle whorls; bone combs; iron tools (knife, sickle, fire-steel); wooden buckets; perhaps a half-finished dugout canoe near water; bark containers; coils of bast rope.

## 6. DRESS AND ORNAMENT

### Latgalian Women's Dress (~9th-12th century)

Based on burial finds and modern reconstructions [11][27][29]:

- **Undergarment:** linen shirt (krekls) with long sleeves
- **Skirt:** mid-calf length woolen skirt
- **Shoulder shawl/cloak (villaine):** elongated rectangular wool cloak in **dark blue** (the characteristic color), richly adorned with bronze ornaments -- spirals, rings, sheet-bronze mounts [11][27]
- **Headwear:** wide, luxurious crown/chaplet (**vaiņags**) with ornate tassels and bronze decorative elements, including spiral bands and chains [27][29]
- **Brooches (saktas):** large penannular or crossbow-type brooches (fibulae) to fasten the villaine at the chest; designs incorporate spirals, solar crosses, and plant motifs [27]
- **Neck ornaments:** multiple neck-rings (sometimes several layered), necklaces with glass beads and cowrie shells [11]
- **Arm ornaments:** spiral arm-bands, multiple finger-rings [29]
- **Small bronze spirals and tin-lead rosettes:** sewn onto fabrics as decoration [29]

### Latgalian Men's / Warrior Gear

Based on grave finds [11][30]:

- **Weapons:** short, broad, single-edged sword (scramasax/sax); **multiple spears of various types** (throwing javelins and thrusting spears); **narrow-bladed battle axe** (later period: wide-bladed axe with blunt end); shield [11][30]
- **Full warrior kit:** combat knife, two-handed M-type axe, spear, javelins, shield, helmet, mail [30]
- **Accessories:** bronze-decorated straps, belt fittings [30]
- **Clothing:** woolen tunic, trousers, leather shoes, cloak fastened with a ring-brooch
- **Trade items in graves:** scales and weights (indicating a merchant class) [11]

### Scandinavian Contacts

Artefacts of Scandinavian origin found at Āraiši; contacts became intense during the second half of the 10th century and early 11th century [8][27]. Weapon and jewelry forms show strong parallels with Scandinavian and Gotlandic material.

## 7. RELIGION, RITUAL, AND DEATH

### The Pantheon

Baltic religion was reconstructed from fragmentary non-written traditions, medieval chronicles (often hostile), folklore, and post-Christianization cultural remnants [31][32]:

- **Dievs (Sky God):** supreme deity, lives in eternal summer sky, creates the world, establishes justice and moral order. Appears as grey-bearded elder checking human moral compliance [31].
- **Pērkons (Thunder God):** the functionally central deity despite Dievs's nominal supremacy. Controls atmosphere, thunder, lightning. Rides a goat-drawn chariot (wheels = thunder). Functions: fertility, justice, opposition to underworld forces. Sacred symbol: the axe [31][32].
- **Laima (Fate Goddess):** goddess of destiny and childbirth, determines length and quality of human life [32].
- **Velnias/Velns (Underworld God):** associated with death, animals, underground treasures, magic, swamps, lakes. Pērkons actively opposes him [31].
- **Jumis:** grain/fertility deity associated with the double-headed grain ear (symbol of harvest abundance).
- **Ūsiņš:** spring deity associated with horses and light.
- **Māra:** earth-mother figure, protector of women and cattle.

### Sacred Sites

- **Alkas/Elki:** the most widespread sacred place type. Alka is "the name of a sacred place or place for burning sacrifices" [33]. In Latvia: 69 named sacred sites registered (in Lithuania: 203). Place-name components: Svētais, Elkus, Baznīcas kalns [33].
- **Sacred groves:** inviolable spaces where no tree could be felled, no animal hunted, no blood spilled except in proper sacrifice. Ceremonies performed near trees (especially oaks), "not to the tree but to Pērkons" [31][33].
- **Sacred hills:** elevated sites combining hill, tree, fire, and water symbolism [33].
- **The World Tree (Cosmic Axis):** branches = sky (celestial bodies, birds); trunk = earth (beasts, humans); roots = underworld (reptiles, fish) [31].
- **No built temples** in the pre-Christian period -- worship at natural sites [31][33].

### Household Ritual

Annual cycle tied to agricultural/pastoral calendar. New Year as cosmogonic renewal ("the cosmos as a living person who was born, developed and died on the last day of the year in order to be reborn again") [31]. Sacrifices at sacred sites for personal/family health, animal welfare, harvest requests, protection from Pērkons [33].

### Burial Customs of Latgalians

**This is critical for the reconstruction.**

- **Flat cemeteries (6th-9th century, western Latgalians):** inhumation in flat graves, pits dug into sandy elevated sites, often near water [6][11][34].
- **Transition to barrows (9th-10th century):** "In the 9th and 10th centuries, the transition started from flat graves to barrows" [6][34].
- **Eastern Latgalian barrows (from 11th century):** hemispheric earth mounds, **4-7 m in diameter**, built under influence of neighboring Krivichian (Slavic) culture [11][34].
- **Orientation by sex:** a defining characteristic -- **men buried with heads to the east, women with heads to the west** [11]. Bodies supine with extended legs and folded hands.
- **Grave goods -- male:** short single-edged sword, several spears, narrow-bladed axe; traders with scales and weights [11].
- **Grave goods -- female:** abundant ornaments, the villaine cloak with bronze decoration, headwear (crown/chaplet) [11].
- **Cremation:** rare among Latgalians; "solitary cremation graves are found, most of them women's" -- interpreted as wives from Lithuanian or Slavic territories rather than standard Latgalian practice [11].
- **Cemetery location (per Radiņš 1999):** 10th-13th century Latgalian cemeteries are located in proximity to water, on elevated sandy sites (mainly hillslopes). Flat cemeteries sometimes placed on sites of earlier settlements [35].

**Assessment of your reconstruction depicting low barrow mounds near the farmstead:**

For a scene set ~AD 950 in eastern Vidzeme, **this is correct but only just emerging.** The 9th-10th century is precisely the transition period from flat graves to barrows. Your scene could defensibly show:
- A few low, fresh barrow mounds (4-7 m diameter, hemispherical) representing the newer burial practice
- Alongside an older flat cemetery area on a nearby sandy rise (perhaps less visible, no mound -- just a cleared area)
- Located on an elevated, sandy hillslope near water
- NOT immediately adjacent to the dwelling (cemeteries were somewhat separate, within the farmstead's territorial influence but at a respectful distance)

If set in the 11th-12th century, barrows would be more established and numerous [34].

## 8. WILD FAUNA AND LANDSCAPE

### Forest Composition (~9th-10th century)

Pollen evidence from the Vidzeme area shows that **substantial woodland remained throughout the medieval period** even near Cēsis [18]. The eastern Vidzeme Upland in ~AD 900 would have been:

- **Dominant conifers:** Scots pine (Pinus sylvestris), Norway spruce (Picea abies) -- these are the timber trees used at Āraiši [12][14]
- **Deciduous:** birch (Betula spp.), pedunculate oak (Quercus robur), black alder (Alnus glutinosa), grey alder (Alnus incana), aspen (Populus tremula), common ash (Fraxinus excelsior), linden/lime (Tilia cordata) [36]
- **Landscape:** heavily forested with scattered agricultural clearings near settlements. Lakes, bogs, and streams breaking the forest. The upland terrain is hilly morainic landscape with glacial features.

### Wild Fauna Attested in Iron Age Latvia

Based on zooarchaeological evidence and historical records [19][36]:

- **Elk/Moose (Alces alces):** present throughout. Major game animal. In late medieval times, distribution restricted to Latvia and more northern/eastern regions [37]. Abundant in the Vidzeme Upland forests.
- **Wild boar (Sus scrofa):** common forest animal, important game.
- **Red deer (Cervus elaphus):** present in mixed forests.
- **Roe deer (Capreolus capreolus):** common.
- **Brown bear (Ursus arctos):** present in Latvia's forests throughout the medieval period.
- **Wolf (Canis lupus):** common predator.
- **Lynx (Lynx lynx):** present in forested areas.
- **European beaver (Castor fiber):** abundant, especially in the lake-rich Vidzeme Upland. Fur valuable trade item.
- **European bison/Wisent (Bison bonasus):** present. Archaeological bone finds documented from the Baltic (Latvia, Lithuania, Belarus) [38]. In the Mid- and Late Holocene, the area comprising East Poland, Belarus, Lithuania and Latvia was one of the few regions where bison was NOT rare (unlike most of its range) [38]. Bison became extinct in Lithuania in the 17th century [38]. In Latvia, earlier (exact date unclear from sources, but medieval presence likely based on zooarchaeological evidence).
- **Hare (Lepus spp.):** common.
- **Pine marten (Martes martes):** important fur animal.
- **Red fox (Vulpes vulpes):** common.
- **Badger, polecat, weasel, otter:** present [36].

### THE AUROCHS (Bos primigenius) IN LATVIA

**This is the most critical fauna question for the reconstruction, given the village name Taurene.**

**Etymology:** Latvian *taurs* derives from Proto-Balto-Slavic \*taurás, from Proto-Indo-European \*táwros, meaning "aurochs" [39]. The Wiktionary entry explicitly states: "vietvārdi pierāda, ka mūsu mežos kādreiz mituši tauri" -- "placenames prove that aurochs once lived in our forests" [39]. The Lithuanian cognate gives us Tauragė (tauras + ragas = "aurochs horn") [39]. **The village name Taurene directly commemorates the historical presence of aurochs in those forests.**

**Timeline of aurochs in the Baltic:**

| Period | Evidence |
|--------|----------|
| Mesolithic (8000-4000 BC) | Abundant. Major game animal in Latvia, Lithuania, Estonia. Bones at Narva/Emajõgi river settlements (Estonia), Lake Lubāns (Latvia), Zvejnieki [40][41] |
| Neolithic (4000-1800 BC) | Present. Aurochs teeth used as pendant ornaments at Zvejnieki and Kreiči cemeteries in Latvia [40]. Still hunted. |
| Bronze Age (1800-500 BC) | **Declining.** In Estonia, "the aurochs population receded by the 3rd millennium cal BCE" [42]. This represents the northern edge of the range contracting. |
| Early Iron Age (1 AD - 400 AD) | **Uncertain for Latvia specifically.** No published bone finds from Latvian Iron Age contexts that I can confirm. However, aurochs survived in neighboring regions much later. |
| Medieval (13th century) | By the 13th century, range restricted to "Poland, Lithuania, Moldavia, Transylvania and East Prussia" [43][44] |
| Late Medieval | East Prussia: survived until at least AD 1500 in the Great Wilderness [43]. Lithuania: implied presence alongside East Prussia [43]. Poland (Jaktorów): last population, extinct 1627 [43][44]. |

**Assessment for the reconstruction:**

The critical question is whether aurochs were plausibly present in eastern Vidzeme ~AD 50 (Early Iron Age). Here is my honest assessment:

**Yes, likely present at AD 50, but the evidence is inferential rather than direct.** The reasoning:

1. Aurochs were abundant in Latvia in the Neolithic (teeth used as ornaments at Latvian sites) [40]
2. In Estonia (northernmost Baltic range), they "receded by the 3rd millennium cal BCE" [42] -- but Estonia is the northernmost margin. Latvia is further south and inland.
3. In Lithuania and East Prussia, aurochs survived until the 13th-16th centuries [43][44]
4. Latvia sits geographically BETWEEN Estonia (where they disappeared early) and Lithuania/East Prussia (where they survived into the historical period)
5. The place-name Taurene itself is the strongest piece of evidence -- it demonstrates local memory of aurochs in those specific forests [39]
6. The Vidzeme Upland is hilly, forested, with extensive meadows near lakes -- suitable aurochs habitat

**My verdict:** Depicting a small herd of aurochs in eastern Vidzeme at AD 50 is **defensible and probably correct.** At that date, they would likely have been rare (not common like in the Mesolithic) but present -- a declining population in the forests of a sparsely settled upland. The place-name evidence specifically for Taurene supports the local presence.

**For AD 950 (the Late Iron Age scene):** Much less certain. If Lithuania still had aurochs in the 13th century, it is *possible* that small remnant populations survived in Latvia's interior forests (like the Vidzeme Upland) into the 10th century -- but there is no direct evidence. I would NOT depict aurochs in the 950 scene without flagging it as speculative. They may have been locally extinct by then, pushed out by forest clearance and hunting pressure. The place-name could commemorate animals that disappeared centuries earlier.

**Elk for comparison:** Elk are safe to depict in BOTH periods. They remained abundant in Latvia throughout the medieval period and are still present today [37].
