# Iron Age Settlement Density Verification: Taurene Parish Tile

**Purpose:** Verify the sim's settlement counts for the AD 50 and AD 950 eras against the archaeological evidence. The sim tile is 8.8 x 8.8 km (~77 km2) centered on the upper Gauja valley at 57.159N 25.665E.

**Compiled:** 2026-07-05 by Cal. Numbered citations at end.

---

## AD 50: ROMAN IRON AGE

### The pollen evidence: forests, not farms

The single most informative source for what the upper Gauja landscape looked like at AD 50 is the palaeoenvironmental study of Lake Araisi (Stivrins et al. 2015, in Vegetation History and Archaeobotany). The sediment core shows that "during AD 1-700, the vicinity of Lake Araisi was covered by forests and human activities were only small-scale with the first appearance of cereal pollen (Triticum and Secale cereale) after AD 400" [1]. This is from a site 25 km southwest of Taurene that would later become the most important settlement in the region. If the Araisi lakeside was virtually untouched at AD 50, the Taurene area was at least as wild and probably more so.

### Who was there: Finnic and Baltic peoples at very low density

The Vidzeme Upland in the 1st century AD sat at the intersection of two cultural traditions. Tarand graves -- rectangular stone-chambered collective cremation burials characteristic of Finnic peoples -- are distributed across "the central and northern part of Vidzeme, in north and central Latgale and the northern part of Courland" during the Roman Iron Age (AD 1-400) [2][3]. Alongside these, Balts practiced collective inhumation in sand barrows [2]. The boundary was porous; neither group was densely settled here.

For Taurene parish specifically, the LNVM (Latvian National History Museum) lists "Taurene Gailisi" as an Iron Age site "reflecting defensive structures and tarand graves from the early Iron Age onward" [4][5]. This is direct evidence of human presence in the Taurene area during the Roman Iron Age. The "Vecpiebalga Ezeni" tumulus graves with stone ring burials, dating to the "early Iron Age and first half of the middle Iron Age," confirm settlement in the immediately adjacent Piebalga area [6]. And the Taurene parish monument register includes "Lejas Libiesu senkapi" (ID 588) and "Libiesu apmetne" (ID 589) -- a Livonian (Finno-Ugric) settlement and burial ground, documenting Finnic presence this far inland from the coast [7].

So the area was not empty. But it was close to empty by modern standards.

### Population arithmetic

Pre-crusade Latvia (~1200 AD) held roughly 155,000 people across 64,500 km2 -- about 2.4 people per km2 at the Late Iron Age population peak [8]. The Roman Iron Age population was dramatically lower. Estimates for Northern European Iron Age societies typically put Roman-period densities at 20-40% of the pre-medieval peak [9]. That gives 0.5-1.0 people per km2 for Latvia at AD 50 -- and the interior uplands were sparser than the coast and river trade routes, which were the primary settlement corridors.

For a 77 km2 tile at maybe 0.3-0.6 people per km2 (interior upland, no major trade route), the math gives 23-46 people total. At 6-8 per extended family, that's 3-8 family groups across the entire tile. But these families were mobile (hunting, forest beekeeping, slash-and-burn shifting cultivation), not all sedentary. Permanent camps would be fewer than total family units.

### Was agriculture happening?

The Araisi pollen record says no significant cereal cultivation before AD 400 in this region [1]. The Taurene area at AD 50 was probably under extensive forest management (fire-managed clearings for wild game, bark-stripping for bee trees) with perhaps incipient barley plots near the most permanent campsites. The subsistence base was hunting, fishing, gathering, and forest beekeeping, supplemented by very small-scale horticulture at best.

### Verdict: 1 camp is correct

The sim currently shows ONE lean-to camp with a campfire, a fish-drying rack, and a dugout canoe near the river. **This is historically appropriate, even slightly generous for a snapshot of any particular moment.** The reasoning:

1. The pollen evidence shows "only small-scale" human activity in the region until after AD 400 [1].
2. Archaeological sites in the Taurene area (Gailisi, Libiesu) confirm *presence* but not dense *settlement*.
3. At ~0.3-0.6 people/km2, any given 77 km2 patch of interior Vidzeme would have had at most a few semi-permanent camps, with much of the population mobile through the landscape.
4. The lean-to (not a log cabin) is the right structure type -- permanent log dwellings belong to the settled agricultural economy that develops later. A shelter of poles and bark/thatch, beside a fire, near the river for fishing, is exactly right.
5. Showing 0 would be a defensible alternative -- a passing visitor might see no human presence at all. But 1 camp establishes the minimum human footprint that the archaeological record documents.

**Do not add more settlements for AD 50.** If anything, the current depiction could be made more ephemeral (a smaller lean-to, fewer props) to emphasize the transience of occupation, but 1 camp is the right number.

## AD 950: LATE IRON AGE (LATGALIAN EXPANSION)

### Population context

By the 10th century, eastern Vidzeme was firmly Latgalian territory. The Late Iron Age represents the peak of pre-crusade settlement, with intensive agriculture, hillfort-chiefdom political organization, and established trade connections [10][11]. Population density for Latvia's territory was approaching the pre-crusade peak of ~2.4-3.9 people/km2 [8][12]. At AD 950 -- two and a half centuries before the crusade -- density was somewhat lower, perhaps 1.5-2.5 people/km2 nationally.

The Vidzeme Upland, with its productive soils, abundant lakes, and forest resources, was not peripheral at this date. Ten lake fortresses -- all in the Vidzeme Central Upland -- attest to the importance of this landscape for Latgalian settlement [13][14]. The Araisi lake fortress, 25 km SW of Taurene, held 50-90 people and its immediate hinterland shifted from forest to "semi-open landscape comprising a mosaic of cultivated land, meadows and pasture" [1][15]. That's the kind of countryside the Taurene tile would also have been.

### Archaeological density in Taurene parish

Taurene parish (102.2 km2) contains 13 registered archaeological monuments [7]. For Iron Age settlement, the relevant ones are:

- **Three hillforts**: Banuzu pilskalns (ID 580), Lejstupu pilskalns (ID 585, on Dabaru ezers), Silenieku pilskalns (ID 590)
- **Three ancient cemeteries**: Nekina senkapi (ID 581), Lejas Libiesu senkapi (ID 588), Zalkalnu senkapi (ID 592)
- **One identified settlement**: Libiesu apmetne (ID 589)
- **One sacred site**: Banuzu Svetavots (ID 582, sacred spring)

Three hillforts in 102 km2 is above the Latvian average (470 hillforts / 64,500 km2 = 1 per 137 km2). Each hillfort implies a surrounding settlement area with farmsteads. The three ancient cemeteries each correspond to a settlement community (people don't maintain cemeteries far from where they live). The sacred spring at Banuzu confirms ritual use of the landscape. This is a well-documented, meaningfully settled landscape by the Late Iron Age.

### How many farmsteads per tile?

The calculation from three directions:

**1. From population density.** At 1.5-2.5 people/km2, a 77 km2 tile holds 115-193 people. At 6-8 per extended-family farmstead, that's 14-32 farmsteads. But much of the tile is forest and wetland, with settlement concentrated on productive terraces. Perhaps 30-50% of the land was actively used, concentrating settlement on the better ground. A realistic estimate: **8-15 active farmsteads on the tile,** with the rest being forest, bog, and sacred/wild areas.

**2. From cemetery density.** In the lower Gauja basin (Livs area), 16 barrow cemeteries occupy ~1,000 km2, or 1 cemetery per ~63 km2 [16]. Each cemetery serves a community. For the Taurene tile (77 km2), that would predict ~1.2 community-associated cemeteries -- consistent with our monument register (Nekina senkapi is within/near the tile, plus Zalkalnu).

**3. From hillfort territory.** Latvian hillforts served as centers for surrounding agricultural populations [17]. The three hillforts in the 102 km2 parish suggest the landscape could sustain about one chieftain's-seat-scale settlement complex per 34 km2. But the 8.8 km tile contains only ONE of these (Lejstupu). That hillfort's territory would encompass the tile and extend beyond it. The associated farmsteads cluster within a few km of the hillfort's view, connected by paths.

**Synthesis:** The tile should contain the hillfort plus **5-8 scattered farmsteads.** This is a Vidzeme-type dispersed pattern (vienseta), not a nucleated village [18]. Farmsteads would be visible from one another across open ground but separated by 200-800 m, each on its own hill or terrace with views of the river/lake and the forest edge.

### The sim's current configuration

The sim shows 1 main farmstead (Araisi-inspired) + 4 outlying farms (ERA2_FARMS array) + Lejstupu hillfort with palisade and a building inside = 6 settlement units total.

This is **at the lower end of plausible but defensible.** The main farmstead and 4 outlying farms map onto the archaeological picture of a hillfort chieftain's community (the main farmstead approximates the chieftain's household; the outlying farms are dependent households). The relationship to the hillfort is correct -- the palisaded refuge sits at a distance, used in emergencies, not as a permanent residence for the whole population [7][17].

### Cluster structure

Each 10th-century farmstead should consist of 2-4 buildings in the scattered Vidzeme pattern [18]:
- **Main dwelling** (log cabin, ~5x6m, bark/thatch gable roof, central clay stove, chimney-less)
- **Klēts** (granary, south of dwelling, possibly raised on posts)
- **Cattle byre** (north side, at a distance)
- **Perhaps a rija** (threshing barn) on a nearby hillock for wind

The sim's ERA2_FARMS each generate a dwelling + either a granary or a second cabin, plus a haystack and a partial wattle fence. This is the correct 2-building minimum. The main farmstead has the most buildings (dwelling, second cabin, granary, cattle shed-analogue). The differentiation between the chieftain's compound and the outlying farms is appropriate.

### Hillfort relationship

Lejstupu pilskalns (ID 585, "Brediqu pilskalns") sits on the western shore of Dabaru ezers, directly adjacent to Taurene village [7][19]. The sim places it at LOC.HILLFORT = {x: -1080, z: 820}. It has a palisade ring (radius 17m) with a gate gap and one cabin inside. This matches the archaeological description: hillforts in this region served as refuge points with wooden palisades, not as permanent towns [17]. The diameter (~34m) is plausible -- Latvian hillforts have plateau areas typically 30-80m in their largest dimension [20].

The broader pattern is: the hillfort is the refuge and ritual/political center; the farmsteads are the residential/agricultural sites; the cemetery sits on elevated sandy ground between them [21]. The barrow mounds in the sim (BUMPS array) are correctly placed as a separate feature, not on top of the settlement.

### Comparison to Araisi

Araisi held 50-90 people in ~16 houses on a 40x40m platform [15]. It was a *lake fortress* -- a fortified permanent residence, quite different from a hillfort-with-associated-scattered-farms pattern. The Taurene tile doesn't have a lake fortress; it has a hillfort refuge plus open-landscape farmsteads. The total population supported would be similar (maybe 40-80 people across the tile), but distributed differently: not concentrated in one fortified compound, but spread across 5-8 individual farmsteads with the hillfort as emergency shelter.

The sim labels its AD 950 scene "Araisi-inspired," which is correct for the building technology (log construction, bark roofs, tongue-and-groove joints) but the settlement *pattern* is correctly different from Araisi's compact fortified community.

## CHANGES RECOMMENDED FOR THE SIM

### AD 50 era (era 1)

1. **No change to settlement count.** 1 camp is correct. Do not add more.
2. **Consider:** The barrowStones in era 1 use BUMPS.slice(0,3) -- 3 stone-marked barrows. Barrow burial in this region at AD 50 is plausible (Baltic peoples practiced collective inhumation in sand barrows during the Roman Iron Age [2]), but the density should be low. 3 barrows for a camp that might be seasonal is at the upper end. Reducing to 1-2 stone markers or making them less conspicuous would be more conservative but is not required.
3. **The aurochs and lean-to are correct.** No changes needed.

### AD 950 era (era 2)

1. **Add 1-3 more outlying farmsteads.** The current 4 outlying farms (ERA2_FARMS) is at the low end. Adding 2-3 more, placed at 400-800m from the main farmstead on separate hilltops or terraces, would better represent the archaeological density. Each needs only a dwelling + 1 outbuilding + a partial fence. Target total: 6-8 outlying farms (plus the main farmstead and the hillfort = 8-10 settlement units total).

2. **Consider adding a second building inside the hillfort palisade.** Hillforts were refuges for the whole community; having one lonely cabin inside a 34m-diameter ring is realistic for peacetime (a watchman's shelter) but slightly underpopulated. A storehouse or provisions shelter would add life.

3. **Optional: add a flat cemetery area.** The 9th-10th century is the transition period from flat graves to barrows [10]. The BUMPS array already shows barrow mounds with stone markers. Adding a subtle cleared area on an elevated sandy rise (no mound, just a few markers) somewhere between the main farmstead and the hillfort would represent the older flat cemetery tradition that was still in use alongside the newer barrows. This is a refinement, not a necessity.

4. **Optional: add a sacred tree/grove marker.** The Banuzu Svetavots (sacred spring, ID 582) documents ritual landscape use in the parish [7]. The sim already has an offering pile near LOC.OAK. Consider adding a second ritual marker -- perhaps a cleared grove with stones -- at a different point, representing the kind of multi-site sacred landscape that the archaeological record documents. Again, a refinement.

5. **No changes to the Araisi-style building technology.** Log cabins with bark gable roofs, wattle fences, post-raised granaries -- all correct for the 10th century.

6. **No changes to the hillfort.** Lejstupu palisade ring is correctly placed and sized.
