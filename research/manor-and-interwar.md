# Vidzeme Countryside Reconstruction: Taurene Parish (formerly Nekenes/Notkenshof)

Research for 3D scene reconstruction. Two eras: (A) mid-19th-century manor period, (B) 1920s-30s independent Latvia. Centered on a Latvian peasant farmstead in the Piebalga highlands.

---

## 0. THE PLACE: GEOGRAPHY AND THE BREZGIS CONNECTION

Taurene parish sits in the Vidzeme Upland (Vidzemes augstiene), the hilliest terrain in Latvia. Average elevation ~250m; morainic hills, elongated subglacial lakes (Alauksts, Banuzu, Kaupenu, Juveris), dense spruce forests, and the upper Gauja River cutting through the landscape [1]. The terrain is a mosaic of fields, forests, roads, and farmsteads on gentle slopes, with forests on steeper ground and in damp hollows [2].

**Brezga kalns** (also called Karatavu kalns) stands at **255.4 meters** above sea level in Taurene parish, approximately 1 km long and up to 450 m wide, composed of glaciotectonic sand, gravel, and moraine. It forces the Gauja River into a sharp eastward turn. An oak tree stands at its summit. The old Cesis-Vecpiebalga highway crossed it [3]. A 1937 photograph in the Zudusi Latvija archive shows a road sign pointing to it from the Cesis-Madona road, west of Lake Alauksts [4]. During the 1920s-30s independence era, the hill hosted annual Jani bonfires, green ball celebrations, and open-air theater performances (1937-1939); these ceased with the Soviet army's arrival in 1940 [3].

The name "Brezga" is a local toponym predating the 1826 surname adoption — it appears in the Vidzeme Upland landscape canon as one of the "augstumi" (heights) specific to the Taurene rural area [2]. This strongly suggests the Brezgis family surname derives from this hill, following the common Vidzeme pattern of surnames taken from farm or landmark names.

## 1. AGRARIAN TIMELINE FOR VIDZEME

**1804** — First Livonian Peasants' Act replaces serfdom with villeinage (still attached to land but with defined obligations) [5].

**1819** — Livonian Peasants' Act adopted (signed into law 26 March 1819, promulgated in Riga 6 January 1820). Formally abolishes serfdom, grants "personal freedom and freedom of movement" to ~300,000 people. BUT: peasants receive no land; they become landless free laborers or tenants [5][6].

**Movement restrictions**: 3 years confined to parish, next 3 years confined to district. Full mobility only gradually achieved by 1830s [5].

**1826** — Special soul revision in the Governorate of Livonia for surname registration. The majority of Vidzeme peasants adopted fixed hereditary surnames in 1826, documented in parish records. In Piebalga and eastern/central Vidzeme, Latvian-derived surnames were chosen more often than German ones (contrast with areas closer to Riga) [7][8].

**1849-1850** — New Peasants' Act declares peasant-occupied land as "farmland" that can be rented for money or sold to peasants. This is the legal basis for eventual farm purchase [5].

**Corvee (klausas) to money rent transition**: The 1849 law aimed to replace corvee with monetary rent. In practice, many landlords continued requiring unpaid labor even after the switch to cash rents was theoretically mandated. The transition was gradual and uneven — some farms paid money rent by the 1850s, others still performed corvee into the 1870s [9].

**1860s-1870s** — Peasants begin purchasing farms from manor owners in significant numbers. By the 1860s only a small number had been sold; the pace accelerated through the 1870s-1880s with credit institutions enabling purchases [9][10].

**Pagasts institution**: The 1866 Baltic Provinces Parish Municipalities Act transferred administrative rights from manors to parish municipalities (pagasts), giving peasant communities elected councils and limited fiscal powers from property taxes [11].

**1905 Revolution** — In summer 1905, revolutionary events move to the countryside. 94% of Latvian parishes elect new administrative bodies. In autumn, armed conflict between German nobility and Latvian peasants in Vidzeme and Courland. 449 German manor houses burned across Latvia. Martial law declared in Vidzeme late November. Cossack punitive expeditions execute over 2,000 without trial, burn 300 buildings [12].

**Critical detail for Nekena muiza**: During 1905, Nekena manor was NOT burned — local peasants and servants stood against the arsonists, reportedly because the Pander family was known for humane estate management [13][14].

**1920 Agrarian Reform** — Constitutional Assembly passes the law 16 September 1920. The State Land Fund acquires 61% of all land. German nobles left with no more than 50 hectares each. Over 54,000 new smallholding farms (jaunsaimniecibas) created, average 17.1 ha. Landlessness drops from 61.2% (1897) to 18% (1936) [15].

**Nekena muiza specifically**: Three estates in Taurene parish were subdivided: Nekina (72 parcels, 1,150 ha), Lodes (38 parcels, 785 ha), and Gustu (25 parcels, 616 ha) [16].

**Parish renaming wave**: After independence, German-derived parish names were systematically replaced with Latvian toponyms. In 1925, schoolteacher Karlis Bormanis proposed renaming Nekenes pagasts to **Taurenes pagasts**, effective 1 January 1926 [14][16][17].

## 2. THE VIENSETA (DISPERSED FARMSTEAD) c. 1860

Based on the Latvian Ethnographic Open-Air Museum's Vidzeme Peasant Homestead exhibit (representing "an average wealthy peasant of Vidzeme, late 18th to mid-19th century") and ethnographic literature [18][19][20].

### Layout

Buildings grouped around an **irregular yard (pagalms)**. The dwelling house at center, built on a dry hillock, oriented lengthwise roughly north-south. Cattle shed to the north of the dwelling. Granary (klets) closer and to the south. Threshing barn (rija) set apart on higher ground, forming its own sub-yard. Bathhouse (pirts) near water. All buildings visible from the dwelling's windows or door [18][19].

In the Piebalga sub-region specifically: "compact groups of farmsteads, where there are 2 to 10 farms close together" with characteristic gray log houses, four-sided roofs, and orchards [2].

### Building Set

**Dzivojama maja (Dwelling House)**
- Construction: Horizontal log (pine or spruce), saddle-notch or dovetail corner joints. First rows of logs resting on fieldstone foundations.
- Roof: By 1860, TRANSITIONAL — straw thatch still widespread but wooden shingle (skiedru jumts) increasingly common on newer or improved dwellings. Thatch remained on older buildings and outbuildings.
- **Chimney status c. 1860**: YOUR DEPICTION IS CORRECT BUT BORDERLINE. Chimneys appeared in some peasant buildings as early as 1750; by mid-19th century, mantel chimneys or proper chimneys were becoming standard in Vidzeme, especially on newly built houses. A middling farm built or renovated around 1860 would likely have a chimney. An older unrenovated house might still be chimneyless (dumistaba). For the 3D scene: **chimney is appropriate for 1860** [20][21].
- **Glass windows c. 1860**: YES, CORRECT. Even the wealthiest peasants had glass-pane windows (in lead frames) from the 17th century. By the 19th century, four- or six-pane windows were standard in dwelling houses [20]. Small panes, wooden frames.
- Interior rooms: Prieksnams (entryway), istaba (main living room with stove/krasns), virtuve (kitchen area), kambaris/kambariti (small chambers/bedrooms). The masonry stove (krasns) served both heating and cooking.
- Dimensions: Typically 8-12m long, 5-7m wide, single-storey with loft (benikis) above for sleeping/storage. (Exact museum dimensions not published online; these are typical ranges from ethnographic literature.)

**Rija (Threshing Barn)**
- The signature Vidzeme building. Often the largest structure on the farm.
- Three sections: drying room (with sunken clay kiln/stove for grain drying), threshing floor (klons), and storage chamber.
- The museum example dates c. 1730 from Vestiena — "a compact clay stove sunk to the ground for safety from fire" [18].
- In Vidzeme, the rija historically doubled as a dwelling (dzivojama rija) — families lived in the heated drying room through winter. By 1860 this practice was declining as separate dwelling houses became standard, but it persisted on poorer farms [18][19].
- Construction: Log, on a hillock, oriented to prevailing winds. Roof: straw thatch (traditional and long-lasting on the rija because the smoke from grain-drying preserved the thatch).
- Vidzeme rijas noted for "symmetry and spacious threshing areas" [22].
- **Your depiction of a big thatched rija: CORRECT.**

**Klets (Granary/Storehouse)**
- Separate log building on raised fieldstone foundations (boulders at corners and middle) for moisture protection and air circulation. The most carefully constructed building — it held the family's valuables and food stores [19][22].
- Locked with an iron lock — the only locked building on the farm.
- Roof: wooden shingle (more protective than thatch for stored goods). **Your shingle roof on klēts: CORRECT.**
- Dimensions: smaller than the dwelling, typically 4-6m x 4-6m, sometimes with two chambers.
- Placed to the south of the dwelling, near but not touching it.

**Kuts (Cattle Shed/Byre)**
- Long low building, two-chambered, windowless [18].
- Left section: livestock (cows, horses, sheep, pigs). Upper level for hay and bedding storage.
- Right section: agricultural equipment.
- Front paddock for summer milking.
- Construction: log or mixed (log frame with board infill), fieldstone foundation.
- Museum example from Adazi, mid-19th century [18].
- **Your "long low kuts": CORRECT.**

**Pirts (Bathhouse/Sauna)**
- Small log building near water (river, lake, or spring). Every farmstead had one by the 19th century; often built before the dwelling when settling new land [23].
- Used for bathing (wet sauna — water on heated stones), also for births, treating the sick, and the dying. Also for flax processing (retting/drying).
- Construction: log, with stone/brick stove, no chimney (smoke sauna traditional). Roof: thatch or shingle.
- The museum example (c. 1815, from Seli) combines bathhouse with a dwelling chamber for a landless servant [18].
- **Your pirts by the river: CORRECT.**

**Pagrabs (Cellar)**
- Dug into a hillside or built as a mound structure (earth-covered log or stone vault) for food storage. Not always a separate building — sometimes under the dwelling or klēts.

**Well with sweep (vinda/svirpsts)**
- The traditional Latvian well-sweep (aka ar sviram or sviras aka) is a counterbalanced lever for drawing water — a long pole balanced on a forked upright, with a weight at one end and bucket at the other.
- Standard on farmsteads throughout Vidzeme. The possibility of digging a good well was crucial in choosing a farmstead site [19].
- **Your well-sweep (vinda): CORRECT. The term "vinda" may refer more specifically to a windlass/winch-type well; "sviras" or "svirpsts" is the sweep/lever type. Both existed. A sweep is more visually distinctive and period-appropriate.**

**Slietenis (Summer Cook House)**
- Pole-layer construction (upright logs, not horizontal), typical of northern Vidzeme, 18th-19th century.
- Used for cooking, laundry, beer brewing, communal meals in warm months [18].
- Not always present on every farm — more common on larger holdings.

### Fences

Traditional Latvian farmstead fencing: the **riku zogs** is a horizontal-rail fence (split rails laid between paired upright posts). Also common: **zaru zogs** (wattle/woven branch fence), and **staklu zogs** (vertical-paling fence). Osier/willow weaving for garden enclosures [24]. The farmstead yard was enclosed; the riku zogs is the most characteristic type for field and yard boundaries in Vidzeme.

**Your riku fence: CORRECT and period-appropriate.**

### Orchard, Garden, Bees

- Apple trees standard; also cherry, plum. Orchards characteristic of Piebalga farmsteads [2].
- Kitchen garden: cabbage, onions, beets, peas, carrots, turnips.
- **Beehives**: Log hives (dobu strops — hollowed log sections, stood upright) are the oldest type; by mid-19th century, plank box hives (kastes) were also appearing. Beekeeping widespread — oak wreaths at Jani were thought to bless bees [25].
- **Flax**: Processed at the farm — retted (soaked in ponds), dried, broken (lauztuve), scutched, hackled, spun. The museum's rija building displays a "flax processing tool exhibition" [18]. Flax and linen were central to the household economy and women's work.
- **Haystacks on poles (siena kaudzes uz statnim)**: YES — hay was stacked around a central pole to shed rain. Typical of the Vidzeme landscape.

### Animals on a Middling Farm c. 1860

Precise numbers are hard to source for "typical" — but from the economic context of a farm paying money rent or recently purchased (20-40 ha arable):
- 3-6 cows (dairy was primary income even then)
- 1-2 horses (draft power for plowing, hauling)
- 5-15 sheep (wool for household textiles)
- 2-4 pigs (autumn slaughter for winter pork)
- Chickens, geese (eggs, feathers, meat)
- 1-3 bee colonies
- Possibly a dog, cats for vermin control

## 3. THE MANOR (MUIZA) ENSEMBLE: NEKENA MUIZA / NOTKENSHOF

### General Vidzeme Manor Typology

A typical 19th-century Vidzeme muiza complex contained [26][27][14]:
- **Kungu maja (manor house)** — main residential building
- **Kalpu majas (servants'/workers' quarters)** — housing for estate laborers
- **Stallis (stable)** — for horses
- **Kuts (cattle shed)** — for dairy herd
- **Klets/magazina (granary/storehouse)**
- **Spirta bruzis or alus bruzis (distillery or brewery)** — often the most profitable estate enterprise
- **Dzirnavas (mill)** — water or wind powered
- **Kaleja darbnīca (smithy)**
- **Ledus pagrabs (ice house/cellar)**
- **Parks (park)** — with linden and/or oak alleys (alejas), often designed in English landscape style

### Nekena Muiza Specifically [14][17][28]

Founded 15th century (first record: 1426, Archbishop Hennings grants "Gerslau" to Johan Frogt). Named Nekena after the von Noetken family from 1601. The Pander family acquired it in 1856 (Guard Lieutenant Augusts Joachims von Panders) and held it until the 1922 agrarian reform.

**Preserved/documented buildings (8 survive today):**

1. **Veca pils (Old Manor House)** — 18th-century building. This is what existed as the main manor house during the mid-19th-century era (your Scene A). **For the c. 1860 3D scene, THIS is the manor house** — likely a modest classicist structure, one to one-and-a-half storeys.

2. **Jauna pils (New Manor House)** — Designed by architect Reinholds Georgs Smelings (1840-1917), construction began 1888, completed after 1900. Neo-Renaissance style with "villa motifs," inspired by Italian Renaissance architecture. TWO-TONE BRICK, two-storey central corps with one side wing, attic residential spaces, cellars beneath [14][28]. **This building did NOT exist in the mid-19th-century scene. It's the 1930s-era manor house.**

3. **Alus bruzis (Brewery)** — 19th-century, on the Gauja riverbank. Produced 25,000 buckets of beer annually. Three vaulted stone cellars built deep into the riverbank for cooling. Beer transported to cellars via rails. **Destroyed in 1944** [28].

4. **Ledus pagrabs (Ice House)** — Where winter-cut ice was stored year-round. Now houses the Taurene Parish Museum; upper floor used for community center and the Evangelical Lutheran congregation [28].

5. **Stallis (Stable)**
6. **Klets/labibas skuunis (Granary/grain shed)**
7. **Kalpu maja (Servants' house)**
8. **Bijusa pasta stacija (Former postal station for horses)**

**Park**: Exists, on the elevated Gauja ancient riverbank terrace. Most farm buildings were built on slopes on both sides of the river [14]. During construction of the New Manor, a Bronze Age burial site (kapulauks) was discovered — Baroness Anna Pander ensured its protection [28].

**Library**: Established 1857 with 300 volumes by Baron von Pander [28].

## 4. THE 1930s FARMSTEAD AND PARISH LIFE

### What Visibly Changed by ~1935

**Roofs**: Shingle roofs standard; some cement tile (eternita) or red clay tile appearing on new buildings. Thatch disappearing on dwellings (retained on older rijas) [20][31].

**Houses**: Wooden but "looking more urban" — plain board-clad facades with porticoes, painted (white or colored). Larger glass windows (multi-pane sash windows). Chimneys universal and standardized. Some new jaunsaimnieciba houses built from plans provided by state agricultural authorities — practical, symmetrical, typically 1.5 storeys [31].

**Jaunsaimnieciba houses on former manor land**: 72 new farms were carved from Nekena manor lands alone (1,150 ha total). Average ~16 ha. New farmsteads built from scratch in the 1920s on previously undeveloped manor fields and forest. Simpler building sets than old established farms [15][28].

**Cooperative dairies (pienotava)**: Nekena muiza itself had a dairy with a 200-liter separator and butter-churning operation [28]. By the 1930s, cooperative processing was the backbone of rural Latvia's economy — butter and bacon were the primary export products. The Jaunpils Dairy Producers Union (founded 1912) is a documented early example [32].

**Parish school**: The New Manor currently houses the parish administration and cultural center. The sources do NOT confirm it was ever specifically a school — rather, the Ice House building holds the museum and the Lutheran congregation space [28]. However, repurposing manor buildings as schools was extremely common after 1920 reform [15]. The school situation in Taurene requires further archival confirmation.

**Roads**: The Cesis-Madona road passed through the parish, crossing Brezga kalns. The old Cesis-Vecpiebalga highway also crossed the area [4].

**Telephone lines**: VEF produced 500 telephones and 400 exchanges monthly by the 1930s, upgrading exchanges in "Latvia's populated places" [33]. Rural parishes would have had at least a telephone at the parish office and post station by the mid-1930s.

**Radio**: Latvijas Radio began broadcasting 1925 with 331 subscribers; by 1940 there were 156,568 [34]. VEF radios won the Grand Prix at Brussels 1935 [33]. By the mid-1930s, radio receivers were spreading into prosperous rural households — a status marker and connection to national culture.

**Ulmanis-era aesthetic**: After the 1934 coup, Karlis Ulmanis promoted an agrarian-nationalist ideology centered on the "Saimnieks" (Husbandman) figure. Mazpulki (Latvian 4-H equivalent), agricultural exhibitions, and grand farming festivals cultivated rural pride [35]. National Romantic aesthetics pervaded public life — folklore motifs, pagan mythology, ethnolinguistic nationalism [36].

**Flag days and song festivals**: National holidays prominently observed; Latvian flag displayed. The Song Festival tradition (dating from 1873) was central to national identity. Parish-level choirs active — three choirs operated in Taurene in the 1920s-30s [16].

### The Lutheran Church Serving This Area

**The Lodes (Apsu) Evangelical Lutheran Church** — located IN Taurene parish (address: Taurenes pagasts, "Lodes Apsu baznica," LV-4119).

- Congregation first mentioned 1536; first Apsu church built 1613 as a Skujenes branch church [37].
- Current building: 1780, preserving features of a medieval wooden chapel. Originally straw-thatched roof, wooden walls, diamond-patterned windows [28].
- 1890: organ by M. Kreslins from Jekabpils.
- 1893: renovation by A. Laumanis (altar, pulpit, vessels); bell cast in Bohumia, Germany [28].
- State-protected cultural monument [16].
- The Evangelical Lutheran congregation now also uses space in the Ice House building at Nekena manor [28].

**Dzerbene Lutheran Church** (in neighboring Dzerbene parish, 3.5 km away): Built 1839-1842, 500 seats. Also connected to the Pander family — the owner of Nekina Manor donated a copy of Rubens' "Christ at the Cross" to Dzerbene church in 1887 (stolen 1990) [38]. Both churches likely served overlapping communities; Nekena residents may have attended either depending on proximity of their specific farmstead.

## 5. DAILY LIFE TEXTURE

### Food and Cooking (Both Eras)

**Staples**: Wholemeal rye bread (the foundation of every meal except porridge meals), grey peas with bacon (pelēkie zirņi ar speki — "even when everything else ran out, dried peas and cured pork could usually be procured"), potatoes, cabbage, barley porridge, beets, onions [39][40][41].

**Bread baking**: Once a week or once a fortnight depending on household size. Up to 18 loaves per bake. Spruce wood for optimal heat. Starter dough stored in a clay pot in flour between bakings. The master of the house cut and portioned bread at mealtimes [40].

**Herring (silke)**: Salted Baltic herring was the primary purchased protein — eaten with boiled potatoes and cottage cheese. A staple especially in winter and for workers [39].

**Dairy**: Milk, cottage cheese (biezpiens), butter, sour cream. By the 1930s, much milk sold to the cooperative; less butter churned at home.

**Beer (alus)**: Home-brewed for celebrations, especially Jani. Brewed from barley malt. At Nekena manor, the brewery produced 25,000 buckets annually — peasants would have purchased or received beer from the estate as well [28].

**Seasonal**: Pork slaughter in late autumn (provides meat, lard, blood sausage for winter). Spring: fresh greens, sorrel soup. Summer: berries, fresh vegetables. Christmas Eve: grey peas, pickled herring, pīrāgi (bacon pastries).

### Clothing

**Workday (19th century)**: Linen shirt (krekls) — home-woven from farm-grown flax. Women: linen shirt + brunči (gathered striped/checkered woolen skirt) + woven belt + linen apron. Men: linen shirt + linen or woolen trousers + woven belt. Feet: pastalas (rawhide shoes) or bare in summer. Woolen shawls and jackets in cold weather [42].

**Sunday/festive**: The elaborately decorated regional folk costume — embroidered shirts, brightly colored woven brunči, silver brooches (saktas), woven belts with symbolic patterns. Each district had its own patterns (similar to Scottish clan tartans) [42].

**By the 1930s**: Workday clothing increasingly store-bought or sewn from purchased fabric. Folk costumes worn for festivals, song celebrations, and national holidays rather than as daily wear.

### Seasonal Rhythm

**Spring**: Plowing, sowing (rye sown in autumn, but oats/barley/flax in spring). Manure spreading (often communal talka).
**Summer**: Haymaking (highest-effort season), tending livestock, garden work. JANI MIDSUMMER at summer solstice.
**Autumn**: Harvest — grain cutting, threshing in the rija. Potato digging. Flax pulling and retting. Pork slaughter. Apple harvest.
**Winter**: Threshing continues. Spinning, weaving (women). Woodwork, tool repair (men). Storytelling evenings. School sessions (historically November-March).

**Jani (Midsummer, June 23-24)**: The supreme rural celebration. Bonfires lit at high points (Brezga kalns was the local Jani fire site!). Oak-leaf wreaths for men, flower wreaths for women. Janu siers (caraway cheese) made by women; beer brewed by men. Singing ligo songs. Jumping over fires. Decorating houses and livestock with birch and oak branches. Properties swept and tidied [25][28].

**Talkas (communal work-bees)**: Neighbors gather for large tasks — harvest, flax processing, manure spreading, building. Host provides food and beer. Singing maintains rhythm. Talka "mothers" direct work. Ends with dancing and revelry. Originally distinct from corvee; by 19th century purely voluntary mutual aid [43].

### Lighting

**Pre-1860s**: Skalas (pine splinters) — split resinous pine held in an iron holder, providing flickering light for evening work (spinning, weaving). Tallow candles for wealthier households or special occasions.
**1860s-1880s**: Kerosene lamps arrive and gradually replace splinters. A major quality-of-life improvement — brighter, steadier, less smoky.
**By 1930s**: Kerosene lamps universal. Some electrification reaching rural areas but NOT universal in countryside until Soviet era. Wealthier farms and parish centers might have generators or early grid connections.

### Sounds of the Farm

ERA A (1860): Rooster crow at dawn. Cow lowing (morning/evening milking). Horse snorting, harness jingling. Flail rhythms in the rija (thud-thud-thud of threshing). Spinning wheel hum. Axe on wood. Scythe sharpening (whetstone ring). Wind in spruce trees. Church bells from Apsu baznica on Sundays. Dogs barking across the valley. Evening: singing (dainas — folk songs during work), splinter crackling.

ERA B (1935): Same animal/nature sounds, plus: cream separator whirring (mechanical centrifuge). Radio crackling from the window. Occasional motor vehicle on the Cesis-Madona road. Telephone ring at the parish office. Sawmill buzz (if nearby). Still: singing, but now also popular songs alongside traditional dainas.
